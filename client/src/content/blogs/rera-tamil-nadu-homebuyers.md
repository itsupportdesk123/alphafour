Buying a home that is still under construction asks a great deal of trust. A buyer commits significant sums to a building that exists only on drawings, and depends on the developer to deliver what was promised, on time and to the standard described. The Real Estate (Regulation and Development) Act, 2016, widely known as RERA, sets out rules designed to make that relationship more transparent and more accountable.

In Tamil Nadu, the Act is enforced by the Tamil Nadu Real Estate Regulatory Authority (TNRERA). This article explains the main protections the Act provides, how to check a project and what to do if something goes wrong. It is a general guide, not legal advice: the law contains detail that this article does not cover, so always verify the position with TNRERA and take advice from a property lawyer on your specific purchase.

## Registration, and how to check it

Under RERA, projects above the thresholds set in the Act must be registered with the authority before they are advertised or sold. Registration brings a project into the regulatory framework and makes information about it publicly available, because promoters must keep project details and registration information on the authority's website.

For a buyer, this is the natural starting point, and a little time spent checking can tell you a great deal:

1. Ask the developer for the project's registration details
2. Find the project in the registration records on TNRERA's official website
3. Confirm that the project name, promoter and location match what you have been told
4. Read the details the promoter has provided and compare them with the brochure and what the sales team has said
5. Note any differences and ask the developer to explain them in writing

If a project that should be registered is being marketed without registration, that is a clear reason to pause before going any further. If anything is unclear, contact TNRERA directly or ask your lawyer to review what you have found.

## How buyers' money is protected

Two provisions of the Act deal directly with the money buyers pay.

First, 70% of the amounts collected from buyers must be kept in a separate bank account and used only for the land and construction cost of that project. The purpose is to prevent money paid for one project from being diverted elsewhere, which helps keep construction funded through to completion.

Second, a promoter cannot take more than 10% of the cost as an advance before a written agreement for sale is registered. In practical terms, a buyer should not be asked to hand over large sums on the strength of a booking form or an informal understanding. If you are asked to pay more than this before the agreement is registered, ask why, and take legal advice.

## Carpet area: knowing what you are paying for

Floor area can be described in several ways, and the differences between them can be confusing. Under RERA, homes must be sold on carpet area. The Act defines carpet area as the net usable floor area of the home, excluding the external walls, service shafts, exclusive balconies or verandahs and open terraces, but including the internal partition walls.

This gives buyers a consistent basis on which to compare homes. When you read a brochure or an agreement, check that the carpet area is stated clearly. If other figures appear, such as a separate balcony area, make sure you understand exactly what each one refers to.

## Defects and delays

The Act also addresses what happens after handover, and what happens when handover is late.

The promoter is liable to fix structural defects or workmanship defects brought to their notice within five years of handing over possession. Keep a clear record of your possession date, report any defects in writing, and keep copies of all correspondence along with photographs.

If possession is delayed, the Act provides for interest or refund. How these remedies apply depends on the facts of each case and on the provisions of the Act and the agreement, which is why legal advice matters if you find yourself in this position.

## What to look for in the agreement for sale

The agreement for sale is the document that turns promises into obligations. Read it carefully before signing, ideally with a property lawyer, and check that it:

- States the carpet area of your home clearly
- Sets out a payment schedule you understand, linked to clear stages of construction
- Gives the date by which possession is to be handed over
- Describes the specifications, amenities and common areas you have been promised
- Explains how defects will be reported and handled after possession
- Is consistent with the project details on the authority's website

Do not rely on verbal assurances that are not reflected in the written agreement. If something matters to you, it should be in the document.

> RERA does not replace a buyer's own diligence, but it gives that diligence something firm to check against.

## If something goes wrong

It helps to know the route before you need it. If a problem does arise, start by raising the issue with the developer in writing, and keep copies of everything: the agreement, receipts, letters, emails and photographs. Take advice from a property lawyer on your position. Under the Act, buyers can complain to the authority; check TNRERA's website for its current procedure, or ask your lawyer to guide you through it.

## Key takeaways

- TNRERA enforces RERA in Tamil Nadu; check a project's registration before you commit
- 70% of the amounts collected from buyers must be kept in a separate account for that project's land and construction cost
- A promoter cannot take more than 10% of the cost as an advance before a written agreement for sale is registered
- Homes must be sold on carpet area, so check that figure in your agreement
- The promoter must fix structural or workmanship defects brought to their notice within five years of possession
- The Act provides for interest or refund if possession is delayed, and buyers can complain to the authority
