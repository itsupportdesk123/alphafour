Every era of architecture answers the same questions in its own way: how to shelter people, how to use the materials and skills at hand, and what a building should say about the society that made it. Modern architecture began as a radical answer to those questions, and it has been changing ever since.

Understanding that evolution helps explain why buildings look the way they do today, and why the best contemporary design in a city like Chennai draws as much from its own climate and heritage as from international trends.

## The modernist break

In the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, new materials changed what buildings could be. Steel frames and reinforced concrete meant that walls no longer had to carry the weight of the building, so façades could open up with large windows and interiors could flow as open plans. Lifts made tall buildings practical.

Architects responded with a new design language. Ornament was stripped away in favour of clean lines, flat roofs, long bands of windows and an honest expression of structure. The Bauhaus school, founded in Germany in 1919, became closely associated with this approach, bringing art, craft and industrial production together. The idea that a building's form should come from its purpose, rather than from historical styles, spread across the world.

## The glass box and its limits

In the decades after the Second World War, modernism became the default language for offices, institutions and housing in many countries. The fully glazed tower, sealed and air-conditioned, became a symbol of progress.

It also revealed the limits of a single international style. A glass box that works in a cool northern climate can overheat badly under a tropical sun, and relying on mechanical cooling to compensate uses a great deal of energy. Repetitive housing blocks were sometimes criticised for ignoring how people actually live, and many cities lost the texture of their older streets to buildings that could have stood anywhere.

These criticisms did not end modern architecture. Instead, they pushed it to become more attentive to climate, place and people.

## India's own modern tradition

India developed its own modern architecture after Independence. The best-known example is Chandigarh, the new capital whose master plan and principal government buildings were shaped by Le Corbusier, with its bold concrete forms and carefully organised sectors.

Indian architects went on to adapt modern ideas to Indian conditions. They used deep overhangs and verandahs to shade walls, brick and exposed concrete suited to local skills, perforated screens to filter light and breeze, and courtyards to bring daylight and air into the heart of a building. This climate-conscious approach, sometimes described as tropical modernism, showed that modern design need not mean sealed glass boxes.

## Dravidian heritage and the Tamil home

Chennai sits within one of the world's great architectural traditions. Dravidian temple architecture, with its towering gopurams, pillared halls and courtyards, organises space with remarkable clarity: a sequence of thresholds leading from the busy street to quieter inner spaces.

![A colourful South Indian temple gopuram at dusk](gopuram "A Dravidian-style temple gopuram at dusk")

Traditional Tamil homes carry the same intelligence at a domestic scale. The thinnai, a raised and shaded platform along the front of the house, gave families a place to sit, greet neighbours and receive visitors without bringing them indoors. Verandahs shaded walls and windows from the sun. Central courtyards drew light into the house, let warm air rise and escape, and collected rainwater. Sloping tiled roofs threw off monsoon rain, and thick walls helped keep interiors cooler during the day.

> Some of the most effective climate design in Tamil Nadu was built long before air conditioning existed.

## Climate-responsive contemporary design

In some respects, architecture has come full circle. Concern about energy use, a changing climate and a better scientific understanding of comfort have brought passive design back to the centre of good practice. Contemporary buildings increasingly use:

- orientation that limits the exposure of large openings to the harshest sun
- façade fins, louvres and screens that shade glass while preserving views
- performance glazing that admits light while limiting heat
- courtyards, atria and stairwells that move air and bring daylight deep into a plan
- landscaping that cools the immediate surroundings
- materials and finishes that resist humidity and coastal corrosion

Green building rating systems used in India, such as IGBC and GRIHA, and the Energy Conservation Building Code (ECBC) for commercial buildings, give designers and developers clear frameworks for assessing these choices. Digital tools now allow architects to test daylight, shading and airflow before construction begins, rather than discovering problems after occupants move in.

## How heritage informs modern Chennai design

In Chennai, the most convincing contemporary buildings translate traditional principles rather than copying traditional forms. A shaded entrance forecourt plays the role of the thinnai, a semi-public space between street and interior. A double-height atrium or an internal courtyard does the work of the old central courtyard. Deep balconies act as verandahs, and screens and fins echo the filtered light of carved stone and timber.

Materials tell the same story. Natural stone, warm metal tones and textured surfaces sit alongside glass, giving modern buildings a sense of weight and permanence suited to their setting. ## Where architecture goes next

Modern architecture began by rejecting the past. Much of its most interesting work today does the opposite, combining new materials and technology with the lessons that earlier builders learned from climate and culture. For a city with Chennai's heat, rain, coastline and heritage, that balance is not simply a matter of style. It is what makes a building comfortable, durable and at home in its place, and it is the standard against which any new development in the city deserves to be judged.
